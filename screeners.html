Step 1 of the wheel. You sell a put and set aside the full strike in cash. You keep the premium either way; if the stock closes below the strike you buy 100 shares at that strike. No shares needed to start here.

Step 3 of the wheel. Requires 100 shares per contract. Returns below assume shares bought at today's price — if you already own them at a different basis your numbers differ.

Buying shares today just to sell a call is a buy-write, not the wheel. In a real wheel you arrive at this step holding shares you were assigned in step 2, at a cost basis you already know. Starting here means taking the full downside of 100 shares up front in exchange for one premium.

Names that clear both screens. The put is step 1; the call shown is a plausible step 3, chosen so its strike sits at or above your assignment cost basis — so being called away completes the loop at a profit rather than locking in a loss.

Both legs are filtered by the same sliders. Widen delta or days to expiry and more names pair up. The call is picked on premium collected, not on the headline if-called return — a distant strike inflates that figure while paying least.

How to use this page

  • Start with the defaults, then widen one thing at a time. The opening view is delta 0.15–0.35, 7–45 days, $5 and up. That is a conservative wheel, not a rule.
  • Sort by clicking any column header. The default is premium return, highest first — which systematically puts the highest-risk names on top. Read upward from the bottom sometimes.
  • Look up earnings before anything else. This page does not track earnings dates.
  • Treat a big annualized return as a warning, not a target. Premium is compensation for risk. A 300% annualized put is the market telling you it expects that stock to move a long way.

Where the wheel actually hurts

  • You keep the premium and the loss. Selling a put caps your gain at the premium but not your downside. If the stock falls 40%, you own it 40% lower having collected 2%.
  • Assignment is not the problem — a broken company is. The wheel assumes you are happy to own the stock. On a name in structural decline you get assigned, write calls into a falling price, and watch the basis you were defending drift further away.
  • Called away caps your best outcome. The stock that doubles is the one you sold at the strike for a few percent.
  • Capital gets stuck. Assigned shares tie up cash until you can write a call above your basis. That can take a long time, and until then the wheel stops turning.
  • Low-priced names are where it breaks. Under $5 there is often no strike above your cost basis paying anything at all.

Three symbols — ADBE, CEG, COHR — were delivered at the 200-day horizon for this session, and they do carry a last price, an IV percentile, a call volume, a market capitalisation and a prior earnings date. What they do not carry is the one number this screen exists to show: the 200-day moving average itself. Without it there is no distance and no percentage distance either, because both are arithmetic on that value.

Why it is missing, precisely. The 20-day and 50-day source views each deliver twelve columns, and two of those twelve share the same abbreviated heading: one is the technical moving-average value, a number, and the other is the vendor's proprietary rating, a word. The 200-day view delivers eleven columns, not twelve. The technical moving-average column is the one it does not select. The single similarly-headed column it does carry holds the vendor's proprietary rating, which this page never publishes — and reading it as if it were a price average would put a restricted rating on the page disguised as a number.

Nothing has been substituted. A 20-day or 50-day average is not a 200-day average, and no value has been computed, carried across or estimated to fill the gap. A table of empty cells would suggest the rows were merely incomplete rather than that the defining column was never delivered, so no table is shown. If a future session's 200-day view selects the technical moving-average column, this horizon appears here with the same columns as the other two.

No vendor rating is published here. The vendor's proprietary buy/sell/hold classification and its accompanying strength and direction ratings are dropped when the records are read. This page carries no column, value, filter, sort key or calculation derived from them.
Methodology, columns and glossary

What this screen is

Three moving-average source views — 20-day, 50-day and 200-day — are delivered for each completed session. They are not merged. Each has its own tab, its own columns and its own counts, and a symbol that arrives at two horizons appears on both tabs. This session delivered 46 rows in total: 20-day (28 rows), 50-day (15 rows), 200-day (3 delivered, no table — see that tab).

How each column is produced

Last
The last price carried in the record for that session.
Moving avg
The moving-average value at that tab's horizon, as delivered. It is a factual number, shown as it arrives, and it is not recalculated here.
Distance
Last price minus the moving average, in dollars. Subtraction of two published figures, nothing more.
Dist %
That difference as a percentage of the average. Negative means the price sits below its average; positive means above.
IV Pctl
Implied volatility's percentile against its own past year.
Call vol
Call contracts traded. Calls only — not total options volume, which is a different measure.
Mkt cap $B
Market capitalisation in billions of dollars.
Next earn / Days / Prior earn
The earnings dates the record actually carries at that horizon, and the calendar days from the session date to a next date. The 20-day view delivers a next date; the 50-day and 200-day views deliver a prior date instead. Each tab shows only the earnings column its own view delivers, and no tab borrows one from another.

What is deliberately absent

  • The vendor's proprietary rating is not here. Its buy/sell/hold classification and the strength and direction ratings that travel with it are removed when the records are read. No column reproduces them and no calculation is fitted to them.
  • Crossings are not claimed. Saying a price has just crossed its average needs the prior session as well as this one. These records carry one session.
  • A horizon with no moving-average value gets no table. Empty cells would read as incomplete rows rather than as a column that was never delivered, so that tab says plainly what is missing and why instead.

How to read it

  • Distance is not a forecast. A price far below its average describes the past, not the next move.
  • Horizons disagree, and that is the point. The same symbol can sit above its 20-day average and below its 50-day. Neither row is wrong.
  • Membership is a slice. What arrives each session is the top of the screen, not the whole screen, so these counts are counts of what was delivered.

Methodology, columns and glossary

The four criteria

Both high-IV-percentile screens share all four criteria and differ only in the earnings rule. The boundaries are deliberately not symmetrical.

IV Percentile
Strictly greater than 50. Exactly 50 does not clear it.
Total call volume
Strictly greater than 5,000. Calls only. Total options volume is a different measure and does not stand in for it.
Market capitalisation
At least $40 billion. Exactly $40 billion clears it.
Earnings window
0 to 45 calendar days inclusive from the session date. Day 0 is inside. Day 45 is inside. Day 46 is outside. This screen excludes any symbol whose next earnings date falls inside the window.

IV Percentile is not IV Rank

They are different statistics and can sit far apart on the same security on the same day, which is why both are shown side by side above. The screen is defined on the percentile. Implied volatility itself is a third number again, and is also shown. None of the three is a substitute for either of the others.

Fail closed on the earnings date

An unusable or stale earnings date never establishes "no earnings". Where a date cannot be trusted the record fails closed for both screens rather than landing in the one whose rule it happens not to contradict. The two screens are complements only over records whose earnings date is known and sound — not over the whole universe.

What elevated IV percentile is not

  • It is not a direction. A high percentile says options are priced for a wide move. It says nothing about which way.
  • It is not a recommendation, and not evidence of suitability.
  • It is not a claim that the volatility is mispriced. Rich options are often rich for a reason that is public and already known.
  • Membership is a slice. What arrives each session is the top of the screen, not the whole screen.
