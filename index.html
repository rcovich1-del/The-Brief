7 Strategy in Focus

Bull Put Spread — Thursday / Normal Regime Feature

NORMAL REGIME

What it is: A bull put spread involves selling a put option at a higher strike (collecting premium) while simultaneously buying a put at a lower strike (limiting risk). The trade profits if the underlying stays above the short strike at expiration — you keep the net credit received. Maximum loss is the spread width minus the net credit; maximum gain is the net credit.

What today's tape does and does not tell you: The NORMAL regime (VIX 14.32, SPY above all key MAs, Fed signaling hold) and the upward-sloping VIX term structure (VXST 11.68 → VXMT 19.78) describe an orderly, non-panicked market — a backdrop in which a defined-risk credit structure is worth studying. That is all it establishes. A regime never qualifies a trade. A bull put spread qualifies only when every condition below has been checked on the actual chain and chart: an IV Rank above 50%, a short strike in the 15–20 delta band placed below recent support, a monthly expiration 30–180 days out, tradeable liquidity at both strikes, and no earnings inside the window. This page shows implied volatility levels rather than IV Rank and carries no strike-level chain, so those conditions are stated here as outstanding, not as met.

Educational example (delta/percentage terms): Assume an underlying at $100 and a monthly expiration about 45 days out. You sell a −0.18 delta put (the $88 strike in this example, 12% out of the money) for a premium of $1.20, and buy a −0.08 delta put (the $83 strike, 17% out of the money) for $0.45. Net credit = $0.75. Max gain = $0.75 per share, or $75 per contract. Max loss = spread width ($5.00) minus credit ($0.75) = $4.25 per share, or $425 per contract. Breakeven = short strike ($88.00) minus net credit ($0.75) = $87.25 — a 12.75% cushion below the starting price. The trade profits as long as the underlying holds above $87.25 at expiration. Return on risk = $0.75 / $4.25 = 17.6% for the period. Note what the lower delta buys and what it costs: a wider cushion and a higher probability of the short strike expiring worthless, in exchange for a smaller credit.

Entry criteria: IV Rank above 50% — an elevated-premium environment measured against the underlying's own recent range, not against the market's; a short strike around 15–20 delta, placed below recent support; a monthly expiration roughly 30–180 days out; the underlying trending above its 20-day moving average; no earnings inside the position's window; and strikes liquid enough to exit at a fair price. Every one of these is a check, not a conclusion — an idea that has not cleared all of them is presented as conditional, and this page has neither IV Rank nor a strike-level chain with which to clear them.

⚠ Main Risk

The primary risk of a bull put spread is a sharp, sudden downward move in the underlying that pushes price below the short strike before expiration — resulting in the maximum loss ($4.25 per share, $425 per contract, in the example above). A gap-down on earnings, a macro shock (a surprise Fed rate hike, a geopolitical event), or a deterioration in the broader regime (VIX spiking above 22) can move a trade against you quickly. A defined-risk structure caps the loss; it does not make it small. Treat the full spread width less the credit as money genuinely at stake, avoid holding through earnings, and set the exit before the trade is on. Closing the position when the loss reaches twice the credit received is not merely a common convention — it is the documented rule this application follows, together with taking profits at about half the credit received well inside half the position's planned duration. Today's AVGO after-hours guide miss is a reminder that even well-positioned trades can encounter rapid reversals on guidance surprises.
