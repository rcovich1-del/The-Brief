06Options Overview

Screen filters applied: IV Rank at or above 40% (the screen’s own floor), earnings cleared with no report inside 14 days unless the trade is explicitly an earnings play, options volume above 1,000 contracts, 3-month relative volume above 1.0, and price between $20 and $500. Structures are described in delta and percentage terms only — no specific strikes, and nothing here is a recommendation.

The standard this page applies is stricter, and is not the same test: a credit structure needs an IV Rank above 50%. For a vertical credit spread the short strike is around 15–20 delta on a monthly expiration roughly 30–180 days out — those are the documented standards for vertical spreads and the related Module 1 structures, and they are not cash-secured-put rules. A cash-secured put is measured against the accepted Wheel boundary instead: a 0.50 absolute put-delta ceiling, and whether assignment at the strike is an outcome you would accept. IV Rank is a credit-structure test only; it neither qualifies nor disqualifies a debit idea. Because the screen’s floor is lower than the standard, a name can be delivered below and still not clear it — each candidate shows its own IV Rank, so the difference is visible rather than assumed.

07Strategy in Focus — The Bull Put Spread

A regime never qualifies a trade. A normal, orderly tape is a backdrop, not a permission: IV Rank, short-strike delta, expiration, strike placement, earnings timing and liquidity are each checked on the individual name, and none of them is satisfied by the market being calm.

Why this one, today. The regime reads normal — VIX at 14.53 is under the 18 threshold and SPY at 770.19 is above its 20-day at 769.05 — so a structure with a bullish-to-neutral bias is consistent with the tape. But index volatility sits in the bottom 5% of its own 52-week range, which means outright long calls are cheap and outright index premium sales pay almost nothing. The bull put spread threads that: it collects premium where premium actually exists (single names carrying IV ranks in the 48–85% band), it defines the loss in advance, and it does not require the market to go up — only to not fall through a level.

How it is built, in delta and percentage terms

  • Sell a put at roughly 15–20 delta. On the names above that lands somewhere below the option-implied expected move. How far under spot that lands depends on the chain and is not asserted here.
  • Buy a further out-of-the-money put roughly 3–4% below the short strike. That long leg is what converts an open-ended obligation into a fixed, known maximum loss.
  • Expiration: A monthly expiration roughly a monthly expiration roughly 30–180 days out out. Shorter than 30 days and the position is dominated by gamma rather than time decay; longer than 45 and capital sits idle while decay is slow.
  • Credit target: Read off the chain. The one-third-of-width figure is quoted for a wider short strike than the standard; a 15–20 delta strike sits further out and collects less, and no figure is asserted for it here. Collect less than that and the risk-reward stops paying for the tail; demand much more and the short strike ends up too close to spot.
  • Management: close at roughly 50% of the maximum credit, or well inside half the position's planned duration - a fraction rather than a fixed day count, because the expiration runs 30 to 180 days and a fixed count does not mean the same thing at both ends. The last three weeks are where a spread that has behaved well can still turn. Set the exit before the trade is on: closing the position when the loss reaches twice the credit received is not merely a common convention — it is the documented rule this application follows, alongside taking profits at about half the credit received, well inside half the position’s planned duration.

The main risk — read this part twice

A bull put spread's maximum loss is the width of the strikes minus the credit received, which is larger than the credit by (width − credit) ÷ credit — twice the credit if the credit is a third of the width, three times if it is a quarter, and more at the smaller credits a 15–20 delta short strike collects. The break-even sits at the short strike less the credit, and losses do not build gradually: if the underlying gaps below the long strike the position is at close to its full defined loss immediately, with no premium left to collect and very little time value to recover. Two specific hazards apply to this week. First, Monday is a holiday, so any position opened before the weekend carries three calendar days of headline exposure — an ongoing U.S.–Iran conflict, oil up 9.26% in five sessions — with no ability to adjust. Second, CPI and PPI land mid-week and are, on the consensus read, the deciding input on whether the Fed hikes at the September meeting; a hawkish surprise would hit exactly the kind of high-beta single names that carry the fattest IV ranks. The defined-risk structure caps what can be lost, but it does not make the loss unlikely, and selling premium into a genuinely two-sided macro event is a decision to be paid for taking that risk, not a way to avoid it.

What these standards cover. The 15–20 delta short strike and the monthly 30–180 day expiration are the documented standards for vertical credit spreads and the related Module 1 structures. They are not cash-secured-put rules: no accepted Wheel record supplies them. A cash-secured put is measured against the Wheel records’ own boundary — a 0.50 absolute put-delta ceiling — and against whether assignment at the strike is an outcome you would accept. A cash-secured put used as the short leg of a defined-risk vertical spread is a vertical spread, and takes the standards above.

No worked example is carried. Working one requires a real option chain — actual strikes, bids and asks at the 15–20 delta band on a named monthly expiration — and this page carries none. Numbers invented to illustrate the arithmetic would be indistinguishable, to a reader, from numbers that came from a market. Build the example on the chain in front of you.

09Bottom Line

A jobs report that beat by more than 100,000 pushed yields to 52-week highs at the front of the curve and left markets pricing a coin flip on a September rate hike — and the S&P still only gave back 0.38%. That is the whole session in one line: a hawkish repricing that equities largely absorbed. Beneath it, the tape was narrower than the index suggests. Three of eleven sectors closed green, one-month new lows outnumbered new highs on both exchanges, and individual names moved 15–20% in single sessions while VIX finished at 14.53. Both major indices remain above all three moving averages with RSI in the low-to-mid 50s, so the regime reads normal — but SPY sits a fraction above its 20-day, index volatility is in the bottom 5% of its own year, and Monday's holiday means three calendar days of exposure before anyone can react.

Top focus: the mid-week CPI and PPI prints. Waller's willingness to hold rates steady was explicitly conditioned on that data, hike odds are near 50%, and index volatility is priced as though nothing is coming. That combination — a genuinely binary macro event against the cheapest index vol of the year — is the single thing most likely to reprice this market, and it is the reason to keep structures defined and small into Tuesday's reopen.
