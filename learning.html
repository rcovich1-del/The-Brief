03 This week

Two expirations, two jobs: what a diagonal is

Week 1 · Foundations · Diagonal Spreads

A vertical spread uses two strikes in one expiration. A calendar spread uses one strike in two expirations. A diagonal changes both at once - two different strikes and two different expirations. That is the entire definition, and every property this month discusses follows from it.

The near-dated option is sold. The later-dated option is bought. They are not two halves of the same thing; they have different jobs. The short option's job is to decay: it is closer to expiration, so the passage of time works on it faster than on the option behind it. The long option's job is twofold. First, it limits what an adverse move beyond the short strike can cost, though not to a fixed figure - because the two legs expire at different dates, the worst case depends on what the later-dated option is still worth at the near expiration, and has to be read off the position's own payoff rather than calculated from the strikes. Second, it is still there, holding value, after the short option has expired or been closed.

Why two expirations behave differently from one

In a vertical spread, both legs experience time identically. Whatever time does to one, it does to the other, and the position's value depends almost entirely on where price sits relative to the strikes. A diagonal breaks that symmetry deliberately. As the near expiration approaches, the sold option loses time value quickly while the purchased option, which still has time remaining, loses it slowly. The gap between those two rates is what the position is designed to collect.

The same asymmetry is what makes the position awkward to describe with a single payoff diagram. A diagram drawn to the near expiration and a diagram drawn to the far expiration are different pictures of the same trade, and a reader who looks only at the far one can convince themselves a position is comfortable while the near-term exposure is anything but.

This is a directional position

It is tempting to file a diagonal under income structures and assume it is neutral. It is not. A call diagonal, built above the market, leans against further upside; a put diagonal, built below it, leans against further downside. Put diagonals require additional caution because markets can continue falling quickly. The lean is deliberate and it is the point: the structure is normally opened after an extended move, in the expectation that the move slows, stalls or partially retraces. If the move continues at speed, this position is on the wrong side of it.

The exposures

Diagonal spread
A short option in a nearer expiration and a long option in a later expiration, at different strikes.
Near-dated leg
The option that was sold. Closer to expiration, decays faster, and defines where the position gets uncomfortable.
Later-dated leg
The option that was bought. Limits, but does not fix, what an adverse move beyond the short strike can cost, and retains value after the short leg is gone.
Theta
The rate at which an option loses value as time passes, all else equal. The two legs of a diagonal have different thetas; that difference is the structure's reason to exist.
Vega
Sensitivity to a change in implied volatility. A diagonal usually starts positive-vega, because the later-dated option it owns is more sensitive to volatility than the nearer one it has sold.
Term structure
The relationship between implied volatility at one expiration and another. A diagonal is exposed to it by construction, because its two legs sit at different points on that curve.
Short strike
The strike of the sold option. The distance between price and this strike is monitored continuously while the position is open, alongside the position's Greeks and its payoff at the near expiration.

Positive vega is worth stating plainly, because it is frequently misread as protection. It means the position generally benefits if implied volatility rises and suffers if it falls. It does not mean the position is hedged. A sharp adverse move in the underlying will usually cost more than any accompanying rise in volatility returns, and the two do not net out in the holder's favour just because one of them is pointing the right way.

This unit publishes no strike or expiration figures. The accepted figures for the short-horizon form of this structure fall outside the parameter ranges this site publishes, and a parameter that cannot be published is left out rather than adjusted to fit. The month therefore teaches the structure, the exposure, the entry condition and the management discipline, and does not teach a number.

What this unit does not establish

  • It does not give an entry rule. That is next week.
  • It does not say a diagonal is currently appropriate for anything. The subject is on the page because the calendar put it there.
  • It does not establish strike distances. Those are specific to the instrument and do not transfer between a broad index and a single stock; the material supplies no rule for converting one to the other, so none is given.

06 How this tab is built

One subject a month, four units inside it, planned twelve months ahead. The subject is chosen by the curriculum. It is not chosen by what the market is doing, and nothing appearing here carries any suggestion that a structure is appropriate for current conditions or for any particular reader.

The teaching on this tab is grounded in an internal, reviewed set of records derived from the source material behind this curriculum. The explanations you are reading were written for this page rather than taken from those records: they paraphrase what the records permit and are worded here for the first time. That wording has been reviewed and approved statement by statement against those records.

Explanations are written for this page. They are bounded by a reviewed internal knowledge library, which decides what may be stated, what may be called a rule, and what must be described as general options mechanics attributed to no individual. Where that material records an absence - a rule the underlying sources never state - this page says so rather than filling it in. Where two accepted sources disagree, no figure is published.

Where a parameter is published, it follows the ranges this site accepts: a short strike of approximately 15-20 delta, approximately 30-180 days to expiration on monthly cycles, and, for credit spreads, IV Rank above 50%. That volatility condition applies to credit structures and is not applied to debit strategies. A parameter that falls outside those ranges is left out rather than adjusted to fit, which is why some months teach a structure and its discipline without teaching a number.

Week 4 of every month is a hypothetical worked example. No archived trade and no case study has been cleared for publication, in any month, so none is shown: the case material behind this curriculum consists of dated individual demonstrations recorded as demonstrations rather than as evidence of expected performance, and republishing one without clearance would invite a reader to treat a single outcome as a result.
