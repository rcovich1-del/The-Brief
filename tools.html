Tools

You bring the numbers. These work out what they imply — cost basis, breakeven, return on risk, annualized yield — the same way every time, with the formula printed beside every result. Nothing here evaluates a trade. Everything is computed in your browser: the page makes no network request and stores nothing. Choose a calculator below; each has its own link.

What these calculators do not do
  • They do not say a trade is good, attractive or qualified. A high return on risk is a ratio, not a verdict.
  • They do not suggest a strike, an expiration, an underlying, or a size.
  • They ask nothing about your finances. There is no field for a total, a percentage of a total, or anything you own, and no figure on the page can be used to work one out.
  • They do not estimate probability. Delta is named as a rough approximation where it is mentioned at all, and never as a probability of profit.
  • They do not account for commissions, fees, taxes, financing, dividends, early assignment or bid-ask spread. Real results differ from these figures for all of those reasons.
  • They do not use market data of any kind. The page makes no network request, sets no cookie, and stores nothing you type.

Results are estimates for education only, not advice: they are arithmetic on the numbers you enter and ignore commissions, fees, taxes, assignment and execution.

Cash-secured put

What this is for: Working out the cost basis, cash required, yield and breakeven of a cash-secured put from numbers you supply.

The formulas used

cost basis        = strike - premium
basis vs price    = (cost basis / current price - 1) x 100
capital committed = strike x 100
static return     = premium / strike x 100
annualized return = static return x 365 / days
breakeven         = strike - premium

Static return is measured against the strike, because the strike is what the cash has to cover. A negative basis-vs-price figure means the cost basis sits below the current price; it is a description of the arithmetic, not an assessment of the underlying.

The formulas used

premium yield     = premium / share price x 100
return if called  = ((strike - share price) + premium) / share price x 100
annualized        = return if called x 365 / days
breakeven         = share price - premium

Return if called is negative whenever the strike sits far enough below the share price that the premium does not make up the difference. The calculator reports that number as readily as a positive one. Annualizing a short holding period magnifies it; see the annualization helper below for why the two annualization conventions differ.

The formulas used

width           = |short strike - long strike|
per contract    = per-share gain or loss x 100

credit spread   max gain  = credit
                max loss  = width - credit
                return on risk = credit / (width - credit) x 100
                breakeven = short strike - credit   (short strike above long)
                breakeven = short strike + credit   (short strike below long)

debit spread    max gain  = width - debit
                max loss  = debit
                return on risk = (width - debit) / debit x 100
                breakeven = long strike + debit     (long strike below short)
                breakeven = long strike - debit     (long strike above short)

Per contract, multiply the per-share gain and loss figures by 100. A credit larger than the width, or a debit larger than the width, is arithmetically possible to type but not a spread that trades; the calculator will show the negative max loss or negative max gain that follows rather than silently correcting it.

04 Wheel cycle

Wheel cycle

What this is for: Following the cost basis and the return across one full wheel cycle, from the put sold to the shares called away.

One full turn: a put is sold and assigned, then a call is sold against the shares and they are called away. Shows the basis at each stage and what the whole cycle returns on the capital the put committed.

Inputs

Cost basis after assignment—
Basis after call premium—
Income yield (both premiums)—
Full-cycle return if called—

Results are estimates for education only, not advice: they are arithmetic on the numbers you enter and ignore commissions, fees, taxes, assignment and execution.

The formulas used

cost basis          = put strike - put premium
basis after call    = put strike - put premium - call premium
income yield        = (put premium + call premium) / put strike x 100
full-cycle return   = (call strike - put strike + put premium + call premium)
                      / put strike x 100

The cycle return assumes both legs run to their described conclusion: the put is assigned and the shares are subsequently called away at the call strike. A cycle that stops part-way — a put that expires worthless, shares that are never called — is a different calculation and this is not it. Returns are measured against the put strike, which is the capital the cash-secured leg committed.

05 Position sizing by defined risk

Position sizing by defined risk

What this is for: Seeing how many contracts a risk budget you choose covers at a stated maximum loss per contract.

A risk budget divided by the worst case on one contract. That is the entire calculation.

Results

Contracts the budget covers—
Risk deployed at that size—
Budget remaining—

Results are estimates for education only, not advice: they are arithmetic on the numbers you enter and ignore commissions, fees, taxes, assignment and execution.

The formulas used

contracts       = floor( risk budget / max loss per contract )
risk deployed   = contracts x max loss per contract
budget left     = risk budget - risk deployed

The result rounds down, because a partial contract cannot be traded. It also assumes the stated maximum loss is genuinely the maximum: for a defined-risk spread held to expiration that holds; for an undefined- risk position there is no maximum loss to divide by and this calculator has nothing useful to say.

This calculator asks for two numbers and no others. It never asks what the budget is a fraction of, there is no field for a total, and neither figure here can be used to work one out. The budget is an abstract amount you supply. What proportion of anything it represents is your business and stays that way — nothing typed on this page leaves your browser.

The formulas used

period return     = amount entered, negative when Loss is chosen
periods in a year = 365 / days
simple scaling    = period return x 365 / days
compounded        = ((1 + period return / 100) ^ (365 / days) - 1) x 100

Both figures answer the question “what rate is this, per year?” and they answer it differently. Simple scaling assumes the return is taken as cash each period; compounding assumes it is reinvested. Neither is a forecast. An annualized figure derived from a two-week holding period is an arithmetic restatement of two weeks, and treating it as an expectation for a year is the most common way this number is misused.

07 Breakeven and required move

Breakeven and required move

What this is for: Finding the breakeven of a short put or call and how far the underlying has to move to reach it.

For a short option: the price at which the premium is exactly used up, and how far the underlying has to travel from here to reach it.

Inputs

Option sold

Results

Breakeven price—
Move required to reach it—
Distance from current price—

Results are estimates for education only, not advice: they are arithmetic on the numbers you enter and ignore commissions, fees, taxes, assignment and execution.

The formulas used

short put    breakeven = strike - premium
short call   breakeven = strike + premium

distance     = breakeven - current price
move required= (breakeven - current price) / current price x 100

This is not a probability. A required move of −8% says the underlying has to fall 8% before the premium is used up. It says nothing whatever about how likely that is. A short option's delta is sometimes used as a rough approximation of the chance of finishing in the money — it is an approximation, it is not a probability of profit, and finishing in the money is not the same event as losing money on the position. This calculator does not estimate any probability, and no number it produces should be read as one.
