Risk & Disclosures

Options involve risk, and they are not suitable for every investor.

You can lose money trading options. Depending on what you do, you can lose the entire amount you paid, you can lose more than you received, and in some positions there is no fixed limit on the loss at all.

That is not a formality at the bottom of a page. It is the first thing on this one because it is the most important thing on this site. Before you trade options you should read the industry disclosure document below in full, understand the specific position you are opening, and be certain you can carry the worst outcome that position allows.

Nothing on this site is a recommendation to buy or sell anything. It is education and general market analysis. It does not know who you are, what you own, what you can afford to lose, or what you are trying to achieve, and it is therefore incapable of telling you what you should do.

03 If you buy options

A long option is a wasting asset. It has an expiry date, and on that date it is either worth its intrinsic value or it is worth nothing.

The entire premium can be lost

When you buy a call or a put you pay a premium. That premium is the most you can lose — and losing all of it is an ordinary outcome, not an extreme one. If the option is out of the money at expiration it expires worthless and the whole amount you paid is gone.

This is different from owning shares. A shareholder whose stock falls can wait. An option holder cannot wait, because the contract ends.

Time works against you

You have to be right about direction and about timing. If the move you expected happens the week after expiration, you were right and you still lost everything.

The further out of the money the option is and the less time it has left, the larger the move it needs and the less time it has to get there — so the more likely the total loss becomes.

Illustration only

You pay $3.00 a share for a $100 call, one contract, $300 total.
At expiration the stock is $99.
The call is out of the money and expires worthless.
Loss: $300, being 100% of what you paid.
The stock only had to fail to reach $100 for that to happen.
Worst case 100% of the premium paid, plus costs.

Leverage cuts both ways and the downside arrives more often than the upside does. Buying more contracts with the same money increases the percentage return if you are right and increases the probability of losing everything if you are not.

04 If you sell options you do not own

Selling an uncovered — naked — option is the most dangerous thing described on this page. The premium you receive is the most you can make. It is nowhere near the most you can lose.

Uncovered calls: no natural ceiling

If you sell a call without owning the shares and the stock rises, you are obliged to deliver stock you do not have, at the strike, whatever it costs you to buy. There is no upper limit on a share price, so there is no natural ceiling on the loss. A takeover bid, a squeeze or a single headline can move a stock far enough overnight to produce a loss many times the premium you collected.

Uncovered puts: down to nearly zero

If you sell a put without the cash set aside, you are obliged to buy the stock at the strike no matter how far it has fallen. The stock can fall to almost nothing, and the loss is the strike less what the shares are then worth, less the premium you received. That loss can substantially exceed the premium.

Illustration only

You sell one uncovered $100 call and receive $4.00 a share, $400.
News arrives and the stock opens at $160.
To close or to deliver, you must buy at about $160.
Loss: about ($160 - $100) x 100 - $400 = $5,600.
You collected $400. Nothing about the trade limited what you could lose.
Worst case Potentially unlimited on the call side. On the put side, the full strike less the premium.

Margin is part of the risk, not a cushion against it. If the position moves against you your broker can demand substantially more money at short notice, and if you do not provide it the broker may close your positions — at whatever price is available at that moment, with little or no prior notice, and without regard to what you thought the position was worth. Uncovered writing is suitable only for people who understand it, can meet those demands, and can absorb a large loss.

05 Defined-risk spreads

A spread caps the loss. It does not make the loss small, and it does not make it unlikely. Defined risk means you know the number in advance — it does not mean you will not pay it.

The maximum loss is real

On a credit spread the maximum loss is the width of the spread less the credit received, multiplied by the contract size. That figure is not a remote tail. It is what you pay any time both strikes finish in the money, which happens routinely.

Because the credit is small relative to the width, a single maximum loss can undo the gains from several winning spreads.

Assignment on one leg

The two legs are separate contracts. You can be assigned on the short leg while still holding the long leg, which leaves you with an unwanted stock position — long or short — and the market risk that goes with it until you deal with it. Exercising the long leg to cover is possible only if it is exercisable, and it destroys any remaining time value in it.

Assignment can also trigger a margin call, and a stock position can move overnight before you can react.

Illustration only

You sell a $100 put and buy a $95 put for a net credit of $1.20.
Width          $5.00
Credit         $1.20
Maximum loss   $5.00 - $1.20 = $3.80 a share, $380 per contract
Maximum gain   $1.20 a share, $120 per contract
Losing once at the maximum wipes out more than three full winners.

Pin risk. If the underlying finishes at or extremely close to your short strike at expiration, you will not know at the closing bell whether you have been assigned. You may be assigned on part of your position, or all of it, or none of it — and you may not find out until the following morning. In the meantime you are exposed to whatever the stock does after hours and over the weekend, on a position you did not choose to hold. Pin risk is the name for that uncertainty, and the only reliable way to avoid it is to close the position before expiration rather than let it settle.

Worst case The width of the spread less the credit, plus costs — and an unintended stock position if one leg is assigned.

06 Cash-secured puts and assignment

Setting the cash aside removes the margin problem. It does not remove the market risk, and it does not make the obligation optional.

When you sell a put you are obliged to buy the shares at the strike if you are assigned, regardless of where the stock is trading. Not at the market price. At the strike. If the stock has fallen well below it, you buy anyway, at a price you can see is worse than the one available to everyone else.

  • The premium is a small offset, not protection. It lowers your effective cost by the amount you received and no more. A decline larger than the premium is a loss.
  • You can be assigned at any time, not only at expiration, if the option is American-style. See section 08.
  • The obligation does not go away because you changed your mind. Once an exercise is assigned to you, you can no longer close the option. You own the consequence.
  • The cash is committed for the whole life of the trade. It cannot be used elsewhere, and the return you are being paid for committing it should be judged against that, not against the premium alone.
  • Only sell a put on something you actually want to own, at a price you actually want to pay, because that is the outcome you have agreed to.

Illustration only

You sell one $100 put and receive $3.00 a share, $300.
You hold $10,000 in cash against it.
The company disappoints and the stock falls to $72.
You are assigned. You pay $100 a share, $10,000, for stock worth $7,200.
Effective cost basis $97.00. Unrealised loss about $2,500.
The premium reduced the loss. It did not prevent it.
Worst case The full strike less the premium received, if the shares become worthless.

07 Covered calls

A covered call is often described as conservative. What it actually does is trade away your upside while leaving your downside almost entirely intact.

The downside is still yours

You own the shares. If they fall, you lose on them exactly as you would have without the call, less the premium you collected. On a large decline the premium is a rounding error. Writing calls against a position does not hedge it in any meaningful sense.

The upside is capped

If the stock rises above the strike you are likely to be assigned and must sell at the strike. Your total proceeds are capped at the strike plus the premium, however far above it the stock has gone. You gave up that gain when you sold the call, and no adjustment gets it back for free.

Illustration only

You own 100 shares bought at $100 and sell a $110 call for $2.00.

The stock goes to $140. You are assigned and sell at $110.
  You realise $112 a share. You forwent $28 a share.

The stock goes to $60. The call expires worthless.
  You keep $200 of premium against a $4,000 decline in the shares.
Worst case Nearly the entire value of the shares, less the premium received — while your best case stays fixed at the strike plus the premium.

There are also tax and dividend consequences to being assigned on stock you hold, and they differ by jurisdiction and by how long you have held the shares. This site does not address them. Ask someone qualified before writing calls against a position with an embedded gain.

08 Exercise and early assignment

Most listed equity options are American-style, which means the holder can exercise at any time until expiration — and the writer can be assigned at any time, starting the day the option is written.

  • Early assignment is not rare and it is not in your control. If you are short an option, you are subject to assignment every day it is exercisable. You do not get a warning, you do not get a vote, and being short only a few days does not protect you.
  • Notice can arrive late. You may not learn you were assigned until one or more days after the assignment was made. In the meantime you may believe you hold a position you no longer hold.
  • Once assigned, you cannot close the option. An attempt to buy it back after assignment simply opens a new position. The obligation to deliver or to purchase the shares stands.
  • Dividends drive early exercise of calls. Stock options are not generally adjusted for ordinary cash dividends, so a call holder who exercises before the ex-dividend date captures the dividend. The chance of being assigned on a short call therefore rises as the ex-dividend date approaches, particularly when the call is in the money and has little time value left. If you are short a call on a stock about to go ex-dividend, assume you may be assigned and end up short the stock and liable for the dividend.
  • Assignment changes what you are holding. A leg that disappears overnight can turn a defined-risk position into an undefined-risk one: a spread becomes a naked long or short stock position plus a single option, with all the exposure that implies until you unwind it.
  • Exercise can be restricted. If trading in an option is halted, exercise may be restricted as well, and holders and writers can be locked into positions until the restriction is lifted.

Any short option can become a stock position without your involvement. Before you sell one, know what shares or cash you would need, know what it would do to your account, and know that you might find out about it after the fact.

09 Expiration

Expiration is the point at which unresolved positions get resolved for you, on rules set by other people.

Automatic exercise

Brokers commonly submit, or accept standing instructions to submit, an exercise for every option that is in the money at expiration by some specified amount. That threshold is the broker's, not yours, and firms differ. An option can therefore be exercised on your behalf even where the transaction costs of exercising exceed the value received, leaving you with a net loss from an option that finished in the money.

The same works the other way: an option just under the threshold may not be exercised, and will expire worthless even though it had value. Find out your own broker's threshold and cut-off time before you rely on either.

Expiring worthless

An option that is not sold or exercised before it expires becomes worthless. For a buyer that is the loss of the entire premium. For a seller it is the best case, and it is the outcome that makes selling feel safe right up until the one time it is not.

If you intend to exercise, you must give instructions before your broker's cut-off, which may fall before the market closes on the last trading day.

Weekend and settlement exposure. Assignment on Friday is typically reported over the weekend, so you may not learn until Saturday or Monday what you are holding — and by then the shares have had a weekend of news to react to. Stock resulting from an exercise settles on the business day after exercise, so the cash or the shares are not available to you immediately. A position that is at or near the strike into the close on expiration day carries all of this at once: uncertain assignment, an unhedged weekend, and a settlement date you cannot accelerate. Closing the position before expiration is the way to avoid it.

Worst case An unintended and unhedged stock position over a weekend, or the total loss of a position that expired just short of a threshold.

10 Liquidity, the spread and execution

A price on a screen is not money. The price you get is the price somebody is willing to trade at, at the moment you ask.

  • The mid is not a fill. Option chains and calculators — including the ones on this site — commonly use the midpoint between the bid and the ask. Nobody is obliged to trade with you at the mid. On an illiquid contract you may have to give up a large part of the spread to get filled at all, on the way in and again on the way out.
  • Wide bid/ask spreads are a real cost. A contract quoted $1.00 bid at $1.40 has a spread worth $40 a contract. Paying half of it twice can consume a large share of the expected profit of a trade before the market has moved at all. On multi-leg positions you pay a spread on every leg.
  • Slippage. Between the moment you decide and the moment you are filled, the quote moves. In fast markets it moves a lot. Market orders in options are especially unforgiving for this reason.
  • There may be no market at all. There is no guarantee that a liquid secondary market will exist for any given option at any given time. If it does not, you may be unable to close a position and would remain exposed — a holder until expiration, a writer until expiration or assignment.
  • Trading can be halted. Trading in an option can be halted or suspended, including when the underlying is halted. While it is, you cannot close, and the value of the underlying can move sharply before it reopens.
  • Open interest and volume are not depth. A contract that traded heavily yesterday may be untradeable at a fair price today. Check the current quote and the size behind it, not yesterday's statistics.

Commissions, per-contract fees, exercise and assignment fees, regulatory fees and financing costs all reduce returns and are not reflected in illustrations on this site.

11 Implied volatility and time decay

An option's price is not just a bet on direction. Two other things move it, and both of them can beat you while you are right about the stock.

Implied volatility

Implied volatility is the market's collective estimate of how much the underlying will move. It is an input to the price, and it changes on its own. When it rises, options get more expensive. When it falls, they get cheaper — even if the stock has not moved at all.

Implied volatility usually rises into a known event, such as an earnings release, and collapses immediately after it. That collapse is commonly called IV crush. A long option bought before the event can lose value the morning after even when the stock moved in the direction you expected, simply because the uncertainty it was pricing has been resolved. Selling volatility has the mirror problem: an unexpected expansion can produce a large loss on a position that looked calm.

Time decay

An option is a wasting asset. All else equal, the time value in it erodes as expiration approaches, and that erosion accelerates as the remaining time shortens. The common name for that erosion is theta.

For a buyer this is a headwind every single day, including days the market is closed. For a seller it is the tailwind the whole position depends on — which is precisely why a seller is being paid, and why the payment is small relative to the loss that occasionally arrives.

The stated maximum loss is a real outcome, not a theoretical one. If you buy an option, expect to lose the entire premium some of the time; that is how the strategy behaves, not a sign that something went wrong. If you enter a defined-risk spread, expect to reach the maximum loss some of the time. If you sell uncovered options, the loss has no such definition at all. Size every position so that its worst permitted outcome is one you can absorb without it mattering, because sooner or later you will get it.

13 Screens and examples are not recommendations

This is the point most easily misread, so it is stated bluntly.

Appearing on a screen is not a recommendation. A screen is a filter. It returns everything that matched a set of mechanical conditions on the day it ran — nothing more. It has not read the company's filings, it does not know why the stock moved, it cannot see the news, and it has no opinion. A name on a list has not been endorsed by anybody. Some of what a screen returns will be there because something is wrong.

Educational examples are not recommendations. Worked examples on this site exist to show how an instrument or a calculation behaves. The symbols, strikes, expirations and prices in them are chosen to make the arithmetic clear. They are not trade ideas, they were not selected for merit, and they are not being suggested to anyone.

A structure being possible is not a structure being appropriate. The site can describe how a spread is built and what its maximum loss would be. It cannot tell you whether you should hold it, because that depends on everything about you that the site does not know.

Nothing here is qualified until every condition is verified. Where this site describes conditions for a strategy, all of them have to be checked before anything is called qualified — direction, volatility where the structure requires it, the expiration, the strike placement, the level it sits against, liquidity, earnings timing and the structure's own risk and adjustment rules. Any one of them failing is enough. The general state of the market alone never qualifies anything, and a page that cannot verify a condition says so instead of assuming it.

If you take one sentence from this page, take this one: a list is a list, an example is an example, and the decision was always going to be yours.
