6Options Overview

Filters applied: IV Rank at or above 40% (the screen’s own floor), no earnings within 14 days unless explicitly an earnings play, options volume > 1,000, 3-month relative volume > 1.0, price between $20 and $500. Structures are expressed in delta and percentage terms — no hard strikes, and none of this is a recommendation.

The standard this page applies is stricter, and is not the same test: a credit structure needs an IV Rank above 50%. For a vertical credit spread the short strike is around 15–20 delta on a monthly expiration roughly 30–180 days out — those are the documented standards for vertical spreads and the related Module 1 structures, and they are not cash-secured-put rules. A cash-secured put is measured against the accepted Wheel boundary instead: a 0.50 absolute put-delta ceiling, and whether assignment at the strike is an outcome you would accept. IV Rank is a credit-structure test only; it neither qualifies nor disqualifies a debit idea. Because the screen’s floor is lower than the standard, a name can be delivered below and still not clear it — each candidate shows its own IV Rank, so the difference is visible rather than assumed.

7Strategy in Focus — The Iron Condor

A regime never qualifies a trade. A normal, orderly tape is a backdrop, not a permission: IV Rank, short-strike delta, expiration, strike placement, earnings timing and liquidity are each checked on the individual name, and none of them is satisfied by the market being calm.

Why this one, today. The regime is Caution: VIX at 15.84 is below the 18 threshold, but SPY closed under its 20-day while holding its 50-day, and a Fed decision sits two sessions away with a hike largely priced. That is a market with no trend and no panic, waiting on a known event — the specific condition a neutral, defined-risk, premium-collecting structure is designed for. A directional structure in either direction is making a call the tape is not supporting. The condor makes no call at all; it simply requires that price stays inside a band.

How it is built

An iron condor is two credit spreads on the same underlying and expiration — a bull put spread below the price and a bear call spread above it. Four legs, all defined risk.

LegActionTypical deltaApprox. distance from spot
Long put (lower wing)Buy≈ 8–10−9% to −11%
Short putSell≈ 15–20−6% to −8%
Short callSell≈ 15–20+6% to +8%
Long call (upper wing)Buy≈ 8–10+9% to +11%

Worked example — in percentages, not strikes

Take a hypothetical $100 stock with an IV rank around 55% and 40 days to expiration. Sell the roughly 17-delta put about 7% below spot and the roughly 17-delta call about 7% above spot, then buy the 9-delta wings another 3% out on each side — a wing width of about 3% of spot, or $3. Suppose the net credit is $0.90 on that $3.00 wing. Then: maximum profit $0.90 (the credit, kept if price finishes between the short strikes), maximum loss $2.10 (wing width minus credit), breakevens at roughly 7.9% below and 7.9% above spot, and the credit is 30% of the wing width. Combined short delta near zero at entry. The two ~17-delta short strikes imply roughly a 66% chance price finishes inside the band at expiration — and that is before any management. Set the exit before the trade is on: closing the position when the loss reaches twice the credit received is not merely a common convention — it is the documented rule this application follows, alongside taking profits at about half the credit received, well inside half the position’s planned duration.

The main risk — read this part twice

The payoff is asymmetric against you, and the direction of that asymmetry is the whole trade. In the example above you risk $2.10 to make $0.90 — you lose 2.3× what you win, and you need to be right roughly 70% of the time simply to break even before commissions. A structure that wins most months is not the same as a structure that makes money, and a single trade taken to maximum loss erases more than two winners. The specific danger is a gap: the condor's worst case is a large overnight move straight through a short strike, where there was no opportunity to adjust or close. That is precisely why the earnings filter exists in Section 6, and why a scheduled Fed decision deserves the same respect as an earnings date — a hike that surprises in its guidance can move an index several percent in an afternoon. The other, subtler risk is that a condor is short volatility: if implied volatility expands after entry, the position loses money even with price sitting perfectly still between the strikes. Position size on maximum loss, not on credit received, and decide the exit before entering, not after the gap.

What these standards cover. The 15–20 delta short strike and the monthly 30–180 day expiration are the documented standards for vertical credit spreads and the related Module 1 structures. They are not cash-secured-put rules: no accepted Wheel record supplies them. A cash-secured put is measured against the Wheel records’ own boundary — a 0.50 absolute put-delta ceiling — and against whether assignment at the strike is an outcome you would accept. A cash-secured put used as the short leg of a defined-risk vertical spread is a vertical spread, and takes the standards above.
